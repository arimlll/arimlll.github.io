## I. The Vital Role of Forensic Judicial Expertise in Criminal Justice

Against the backdrop of the deepening reform of the trial-centered criminal litigation system, the principle of adjudication based on evidence has become increasingly prominent. As a vital bridge connecting medical science with legal adjudication, forensic judicial expertise plays an indispensable role in criminal justice activities. It directly bears upon substantive determinations such as the criminal responsibility capacity of suspects, the severity of victims' injuries, and the causes of death, thereby exerting a profound impact on verdicts concerning guilt versus innocence, and the gravity of offenses and penalties. 

Judges and jurors generally do not possess the specialized medical knowledge required to make these determinations independently, and must rely on expert appraisers possessing specialized knowledge to distinguish and assess facts through science and technology. Expert opinions issued by designated or commissioned appraisers constitute a statutory form of evidence under Chinese law, and frequently carry decisive weight in determining facts and imposing sentences.

However, a succession of shocking miscarriages of justice—such as the She Xianglin case, the Nie Shubin case, and the Hugjiltu case—serve as grave warnings that an expert opinion marred by flaws or outright errors is sufficient to shake the very foundations of judicial justice. At present, there remains a notable gap between the practical operation of medical judicial expertise in China and the requirements of modern, precise criminal justice in the new era. Existing legislation exhibits significant shortcomings in regulating appraisal activities, safeguarding the rights of parties, and interfacing with procedural litigation. 

From the perspective of criminal justice, this paper examines the practical dilemmas and legislative limitations confronting forensic judicial expertise. Taking the inclusion of the *Judicial Expertise Law* in the legislative agenda of the National People's Congress as an opportunity, this study proposes systematic pathways of refinement to ensure this system becomes a steadfast bulwark safeguarding substantive and procedural justice.

## II. Practical Dilemmas of Current Medical Judicial Expertise

### (1) Monopoly of Decision-making Power to Initiate Expertise by Judicial Organs

Under the Chinese *Criminal Procedure Law* and related provisions, the authority to initiate criminal judicial expertise rests predominantly in the hands of investigation, procuratorate, and trial organs, exemplifying an ex-officio inquisitorial model. Although criminal suspects, defendants, and their defense counsel may submit applications for appraisal, the discretion to initiate resides entirely with the organ handling the case, and there is a conspicuous lack of effective legal remedies against decisions to dismiss such applications. This imbalance is particularly pronounced in forensic psychiatric evaluations regarding criminal responsibility.

In the Qiu Xinghua case, which provoked widespread public and academic concern, the defense made repeated applications for psychiatric appraisal backed by appeals from multiple specialists; nevertheless, the court of second instance rejected the application on the grounds that "no persuasive evidence was submitted," ultimately sustaining the original conviction and carrying out the death penalty. This judgment reveals a paradox: litigants and their lawyers typically lack specialized medical expertise and find it difficult to produce "persuasive evidence" meeting judicial thresholds independently, whereas the authorities monopolize the decision-making authority over initiating appraisal. This asymmetrical mechanism risks improperly rejecting appraisals that ought to be conducted, potentially subjecting individuals who are in fact mentally ill to criminal responsibility, in serious derogation of statutory principles and humanitarian spirits.

### (2) The Persistent Shadow of "Self-Investigation and Self-Appraisal"

While the practice of appraisal institutions internal to public security organs providing expertise for their own investigatory activities has long seemed commonplace, utilizing expert opinions produced by internal institutions as courtroom evidence to convict and sentence defendants is profoundly problematic.

Although Article 29 of the *Criminal Procedure Law* explicitly stipulates that investigators who have served as appraisers in the case shall recuse themselves, internal appraisal personnel and investigators remain embedded within the same organization and administrative hierarchy. Driven by the institutional objective of combating crime, expert opinions may consciously or unconsciously tilt in favor of prosecution theories while neglecting exculpatory or questionable points favorable to the suspect, thereby compromising institutional neutrality. Many wrongful convictions in practice reveal that flawed appraisal opinions originated in internal institutions during the preliminary investigation phase. "Self-investigation and self-appraisal" not only undermines the objectivity and impartiality of expert opinions but also erodes public confidence in judicial appraisal.

### (3) Formalism in Cross-Examination of Expert Opinions

Cloaked in the mantle of specialized science, expert opinions readily induce blind trust or deference among judges, resulting in a laxity of substantive judicial scrutiny. Although Article 192 of the *Criminal Procedure Law* established the system of appraisers testifying in court and enacted the rule that opinions cannot serve as the basis of judgment if appraisers refuse to testify without legitimate cause, the actual courtroom appearance rate of appraisers remains remarkably low. On one hand, courts exhibit little proactive enthusiasm in summoning appraisers due to concerns over trial efficiency and cost; on the other hand, statutory thresholds for "necessity of appearance" remain ambiguous, granting judges excessive discretionary leeway.

Furthermore, while Article 197 entitles defendants and defenders to apply for "persons with specialized knowledge" (expert assistants) to appear and present opinions, the legal standing and procedural rights (such as file-review and investigative powers) of expert assistants lack explicit statutory grounding. Compounded by limited recruitment channels and high costs, the expert assistant system fails to exert its intended oversight. The hollowing out of cross-examination makes technical errors difficult to uncover in court, leaving the admissibility of opinions heavily dependent on the unilateral subjective appraisal of the trial judge.

### (4) Absence of Remedies for Litigants' Rights

As previously discussed, from initiation, the selection of appraisal institutions and experts, to receiving opinions, raising objections, requesting re-appraisals, and calling expert assistants, the defense remains consistently positioned in a passive, disadvantaged posture. In practice, investigative organs occasionally notify the defense only of the final conclusion without serving the complete written opinion, making it arduous for parties to scrutinize methodology and process. The absence of procedural safeguards runs counter to modern rule-of-law principles of human rights protection and equality of arms.

## III. Limitations of the Existing Legal Framework

Although the *Decision of the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress on the Administration of Judicial Expertise* was adopted in 2005 and amended in 2015, specific implementation provisions governing medical judicial expertise remain scattered across the *Criminal Procedure Law* and various departmental regulations issued by the Supreme People's Court, Supreme People's Procuratorate, Ministry of Justice, and Ministry of Public Security, lacking unified overarching coordination.

From a legislative philosophy perspective, the existing normative system exhibits a tendency toward "emphasizing administrative management over litigation process." Substantial legislative and administrative resources are funneled into entry qualifications, registration, and administrative supervision of appraisal bodies, while expending minimal attention on refining evidence presentation, cross-examination, and judicial scrutiny during trial.

In terms of jurisdiction and scope, dual-track fragmentation persists. The 2005 *Decision* primarily governs public-facing judicial expertise institutions, leaving internal appraisal bodies within investigative departments largely exempt. This creates two parallel tracks subject to disparate admission standards, operating procedures, and supervisory measures, diluting the uniformity and authority of national judicial standards.

## IV. The Opportunity of Formulating the Judicial Expertise Law

Currently, the drafting of the *Judicial Expertise Law* has been officially included in the legislative plan of the Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress. Drawing together scholarly insights, this article highlights the following directions:

### (1) Establishing the Judicial Expertise Law as Fundamental Law
China urgently needs a coherent, high-ranking *Judicial Expertise Law* that articulates the legal nature and foundational principles of expertise, unifies administrative structures, defines rights and obligations, and establishes evidentiary application rules in litigation, while superseding discordant administrative edicts.

### (2) Reinforcing Litigation-Oriented Reform and Defense Participation
Legislation should enhance procedural participation for parties. Where feasible, expert assistants engaged by the defense should be permitted to witness specific appraisal procedures (e.g., autopsies or psychiatric evaluations), or at minimum be guaranteed full access to appraisal materials. The initiation mechanism must be reformed by establishing review mechanisms when applications are rejected, and setting more permissive thresholds for psychiatric evaluation in capital cases. Courtroom appearance of appraisers should become the general rule rather than the rare exception.

### (3) Breaking the Barrier of "Self-Investigation and Self-Appraisal"
In the short term, institutional constraints, recusal enforcement, and robust cross-examination must be tightened for internal appraisal organs. In the long term, legislation should advance institutional independence or require that expert opinions presented as trial evidence be issued exclusively by accredited independent institutions (such as university forensic science centers or psychiatric medical centers).

## Conclusion

Forensic judicial expertise is the bridge linking medical science with legal judgment; its quality and fairness bear directly upon the substantive and procedural justice of criminal law. Future legislative reform must return to the evidential essence of judicial appraisal, anchoring institutional credibility in science, transparency, and procedural equilibrium.
