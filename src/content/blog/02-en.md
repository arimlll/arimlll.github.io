## I. The Vital Role of Forensic Judicial Expertise in Criminal Justice

Against the backdrop of the deepening reform of the trial-centered criminal litigation system, the status of the principle of adjudication based on evidence has become increasingly prominent. As a bridge connecting medical science with legal adjudication, forensic judicial expertise plays an indispensable role in criminal justice activities, directly bearing upon substantive determinations such as the criminal responsibility capacity of suspects, the evaluation of victims' injuries, and the causes of death, thereby exerting a profound impact on verdicts concerning guilt versus innocence, and the gravity of offenses and penalties. Judges or people's jurors generally do not possess the specialized medical knowledge required to make these legal determinations independently, and must rely on appraisers possessing specialized knowledge to distinguish and assess facts through science and technology. Expert opinions issued by designated or commissioned appraisers constitute a statutory form of evidence under Chinese law, and frequently carry decisive weight in determining case facts and imposing sentences.

However, a succession of shocking miscarriages of justice - such as the She Xianglin case, the Nie Shubin case, and the Hugjiltu case - serve as grave warnings that an expert opinion marred by flaws or outright errors is sufficient to shake the very foundations of judicial justice. At present, there remains a notable gap between the practical operation of medical judicial expertise in China and the requirements of modern, precise criminal justice in the new era. Existing legislation exhibits significant shortcomings in regulating appraisal activities, safeguarding the rights of parties, and interfacing with procedural litigation. From the perspective of criminal justice, this paper examines the practical dilemmas and legislative limitations confronting forensic judicial expertise. Taking the inclusion of the *Judicial Expertise Law* in the legislative agenda of the National People's Congress as an opportunity, this study proposes systematic pathways of refinement to ensure this system becomes a steadfast bulwark safeguarding substantive and procedural justice.

## II. Practical Dilemmas of Current Medical Judicial Expertise

### (1) Monopoly of Decision-making Power to Initiate Expertise by Judicial Organs

Under the Chinese *Criminal Procedure Law* and related provisions, the authority to initiate criminal judicial expertise rests predominantly in the hands of investigation, procuratorate, and trial organs, exemplifying a typical inquisitorial ex-officio model. Although criminal suspects, defendants, and their defense counsel may submit applications for appraisal, the discretion over whether to actually initiate expertise resides entirely with the organ handling the case, and there is a conspicuous lack of effective legal remedies against decisions to dismiss such applications. This issue is particularly pronounced in forensic psychiatric evaluations regarding criminal responsibility.

In the Qiu Xinghua case, which provoked widespread public and academic concern, the defense made repeated applications for psychiatric appraisal backed by appeals from multiple specialists; nevertheless, the court of second instance rejected the application on the grounds that "no persuasive evidence was submitted," ultimately sustaining the original conviction and carrying out the death penalty. This judgment reflects a paradox in judicial practice: litigants and their defense lawyers typically lack specialized medical expertise and find it difficult to produce "persuasive evidence" meeting judicial thresholds independently, whereas the organs possessing specialized resources and investigative powers monopolize the decision-making authority over initiating appraisal. This asymmetrical allocation of rights risks improperly rejecting appraisals that ought to be conducted, potentially subjecting individuals who are in fact mentally ill and lack criminal responsibility to criminal punishment, in serious derogation of statutory principles of proportionate culpability and humanitarian spirits.

### (2) The Persistent Shadow of "Self-Investigation and Self-Appraisal"

While the practice of appraisal institutions internal to public security organs providing expertise for their own investigatory activities may seem commonplace, utilizing expert opinions produced by internal institutions as courtroom evidence to convict and sentence defendants is profoundly inappropriate.

Although Article 29 of the *Criminal Procedure Law* explicitly stipulates that investigators who have served as appraisers in the case shall recuse themselves, and parties and their legal representatives have the right to challenge them, internal appraisal personnel and investigators remain embedded within the same unit and the same administrative system. Driven by the innate occupational objective of combating crime, expert opinions may consciously or unconsciously tilt in favor of supporting prosecution theories while neglecting exculpatory clues or questionable points favorable to the suspect, making it difficult to guarantee institutional neutrality. Multiple wrongful convictions exposed in practice reveal that flawed appraisal opinions frequently originated in internal appraisal institutions during the investigation phase. Thus, "self-investigation and self-appraisal" not only damages the objective impartiality of expert opinions but also severely erodes public confidence in judicial appraisal, posing a grave latent threat to criminal justice.

### (3) Formalism in Cross-Examination of Expert Opinions

Cloaked in the mantle of specialized science, expert opinions readily induce blind trust or psychological deference among judges, resulting in a laxity of substantive judicial scrutiny. Although Article 192 of the *Criminal Procedure Law* established the system of appraisers testifying in court and enacted the mandatory rule that opinions cannot serve as the basis of judgment if appraisers refuse to testify without legitimate cause, the actual courtroom appearance rate of appraisers remains remarkably low in practice. The prevalence of "dossier-centrism" (reliance on written case files) frequently causes courtroom trials to degenerate into the mere perfunctory reading of written appraisal conclusions, making it exceedingly arduous for the defense to effectively question critical issues such as the scientific validity, procedural regularity, and authenticity of foundation materials.

Furthermore, Article 197 of the *Criminal Procedure Law* entitles defendants and defense counsel to apply for "persons with specialized knowledge" (expert assistants) to appear in court and present opinions on appraisal conclusions. However, the legal standing and procedural rights of expert assistants - such as rights to inspect case files and conduct investigations - lack explicit statutory grounding. Compounded by limited recruitment channels and prohibitive costs in reality, the expert assistant system struggles to fully exert its intended functions of oversight and adversarial challenge in practice. The hollowing out of cross-examination procedures makes it difficult for flaws in expert opinions to be detected and rectified in court in a timely manner, leaving their evidentiary weight almost entirely dependent on the free evaluation and subjective impression of judges, thereby greatly multiplying the risk of wrongful convictions.

### (4) Absence of Remedies for Litigants' Rights

As previously discussed, from the initiation of appraisal, the selection of appraisal institutions and experts, to receiving appraisal opinions, raising objections, applying for re-appraisals, and calling expert assistants to appear at trial, the defense remains consistently positioned in a passive, disadvantaged posture, facing a severe shortage of remedial channels. In practice, case-handling organs frequently notify the defense only of the final appraisal conclusion without serving the complete written appraisal opinion, making it arduous for parties and their defense counsel to understand the appraisal process, methodology, and empirical foundations, thereby virtually neutralizing their statutory rights to raise objections and apply for supplementary or re-appraisals. The absence of effective remedial avenues leaves the potential abuse of power in judicial appraisal procedures unchecked, preventing the legitimate rights and interests of litigants from receiving proper procedural guarantees.

## III. Limitations of the Existing Legal Framework

Although the *Decision of the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress on the Administration of Judicial Expertise* (hereinafter referred to as the *Decision*) was adopted in 2005 and amended in 2015, specific implementation provisions governing medical judicial expertise in China remain scattered across the *Criminal Procedure Law* and various departmental regulations or normative documents formulated by the Supreme People's Court, the Supreme People's Procuratorate, the Ministry of Justice, and the Ministry of Public Security, lacking unified coordination under dedicated legislation. This fragmented, multi-agency legislative model has inherently sown seeds of statutory conflict and operational discord.

From a legislative philosophy perspective, the existing normative system exhibits a distinct tendency toward "emphasizing administrative management over litigation process." Substantial legislative and administrative resources are invested in administrative regulatory links such as entry qualifications, registration, roster compilation, and practice supervision of appraisal institutions and personnel. However, judicial appraisal fundamentally exists to serve litigation activities, and its quality must ultimately be demonstrated through rigorous processes of evidence presentation, cross-examination, and judicial scrutiny during litigation procedures. Existing legislation devotes minimal attention to addressing the relationship between expert opinions and litigation procedures or refining courtroom cross-examination rules.

In terms of rights allocation and procedural design, official ex-officio inquisitorial overtones remain excessively dominant, resulting in a conspicuous lack of safeguards for litigants' rights to procedural participation. While existing law endows judicial organs with highly authoritative decision-making powers, it fails to construct a corresponding system of litigants' rights and remedial procedures to balance such power. Litigants' rights to apply for appraisal initiation, their right to know the appraisal process, their right to raise objections, and their right to procedural remedies are either omitted from explicit statutory provisions or lack operable implementation pathways and safeguard measures. Consequently, appraisal activities have largely become unilateral acts of case-handling authorities, leaving litigants alienated from the procedure, unable to participate effectively or exercise oversight.

Regarding the scope of jurisdiction and application, notable statutory lacunae persist. On one hand, the 2005 *Decision* primarily governs public-facing judicial expertise institutions, leaving internal appraisal institutions within investigative departments largely exempt. This has created a dual-track parallelism where social appraisal institutions managed by judicial administrative authorities operate alongside internal appraisal bodies self-managed by investigative organs. Disparities between these two tracks in appraiser qualification thresholds, procedural standards, and supervision mechanisms substantially erode the uniformity and normative authority of national judicial appraisal standards. On the other hand, numerous concrete procedural issues concerning appraisals under the *Criminal Procedure Law* - such as the legal nature and validity of pre-trial appraisals, rules governing the collection and preservation of appraisal materials, explicit catalogs of rights and obligations for appraisers and expert assistants, and procedures for revoking flawed appraisal opinions - conspicuously lack clear statutory guidance.

## IV. The Opportunity of Formulating the Judicial Expertise Law

Currently, the drafting of the *Judicial Expertise Law* has been formally included in the legislative agenda of the Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress. Drawing together scholarly insights, this article puts forward the following systematic reflections on the future *Judicial Expertise Law*:

### (1) Establishing the Judicial Expertise Law as Fundamental Law

Fragmented, multi-agency legislation cannot satisfy the rigorous demands of judicial practice; China urgently requires a coherent, hierarchically authoritative *Judicial Expertise Law*. This legislation should clearly articulate the legal nature and foundational principles of judicial expertise, comprehensively stipulate the administrative management structure, define the rights and obligations of appraisal institutions and experts, codify procedural appraisal rules, and establish detailed norms for applying expert opinions in litigation. Simultaneously, it must systematically clean up scattered departmental rules and repeal provisions that contradict the *Judicial Expertise Law*. Furthermore, the *Judicial Expertise Law* must interface effectively with related statutes such as the *Criminal Procedure Law*, clearly delineating their respective jurisdictional boundaries.

### (2) Reinforcing Litigation-Oriented Reform and Safeguarding Defense Participation

First, legislation should substantially expand litigants' rights of participation in appraisal procedures. For instance, wherever feasible, expert assistants retained by the defense should be permitted to witness specific appraisal procedures (such as autopsies and psychiatric examinations), or at minimum be guaranteed full access to complete foundation appraisal materials along with the right to submit written observations.

Second, legislation should reform the initiation mechanism for appraisal. While preserving the decision-making authority of judicial organs, it must refine litigants' application rights and remedial channels, establishing a formal reconsideration mechanism when applications are rejected; alternatively, it should mandate that judicial organs cannot reject reasonable applications without justifiable grounds, and must explicitly articulate their reasons when rejecting them.

Third, regarding psychiatric evaluations in major cases involving criminal responsibility capacity or potential death penalties, legislation should establish more accessible initiation thresholds. Furthermore, as some scholars advocate, mandatory psychiatric judicial appraisal should be instituted across the board for all defendants potentially facing the death penalty.

Fourth, the system of appraisers testifying in court and the expert assistant system must be thoroughly improved and enforced, with detailed definitions of circumstances deemed "necessary to appear in court." Alternatively, legislation should establish courtroom appearance of appraisers as the general rule and non-appearance as the rare exception, requiring courts to provide written justifications whenever they deem appearance unnecessary. Concurrently, expert assistants should be granted clear litigation status and necessary procedural rights, and a public legal service mechanism for expert assistants should be explored to ensure that economically disadvantaged litigants can fully exercise their procedural litigation rights.

### (3) Breaking the Barrier of "Self-Investigation and Self-Appraisal" and Strengthening Institutional Neutrality

In the short term, procedural constraints and supervision over internal appraisal bodies within investigative departments must be reinforced: the appraiser recusal system should be refined, notification and reason-giving obligations regarding appraisal opinions must be clarified, substantive cross-examination of expert opinions in court must be promoted, and the rights of litigants, defense counsel, and expert assistants to challenge opinions must be genuinely safeguarded.

In the long term, legislation can explore institutional independence mechanisms for appraisal departments within public security organs, or progressively entrust forensic appraisal in criminal cases to neutral third-party independent social institutions. Furthermore, this article maintains that appraisal opinions issued by internal appraisal bodies of investigative organs should solely serve as investigative references and factual clues for investigators during the investigation phase; expert opinions introduced as courtroom evidence for conviction and sentencing must exclusively be issued by independent social institutions with statutory judicial expertise qualifications, such as university forensic expertise centers, judicial appraisal research institutes, and psychiatric health center appraisal institutes.

## Conclusion

Forensic judicial expertise is the bridge linking medical science with legal adjudication; its quality and impartiality directly determine the substantive and procedural justice of criminal law. At present, China's practical operation remains subject to multiple institutional constraints, including inquisitorial traditions and inadequate safeguards for individual rights. Future reform and legislation must return to the evidential essence of judicial appraisal in litigation. Taking the formulation of the *Judicial Expertise Law* as a historic opportunity, China should promote unified and standardized appraisal administration, guarantee litigants' effective participation and confrontation rights, and resolve the structural dilemma of "self-investigation and self-appraisal." By constructing a medical judicial expertise system that respects scientific laws, embodies the spirit of the rule of law, efficiently serves litigation, and robustly safeguards human rights, this system can truly become a cornerstone safeguarding fairness and justice in criminal justice, rather than a breeding ground for injustice and wrongful convictions.
